TESSERA × UKBMS Field note 04 — Habitat embeddings

Does a satellite embedding know good butterfly habitat when it sees it?

Testing a general-purpose land-embedding model against fifty years of UK butterfly monitoring data — and the two ways this result could have been fake.

The finding in one sentence. Shown only a satellite fingerprint of a patch of land, a classifier can tell a site butterfly surveyors chose from a square picked at random 76% of the time. Guessing blindly would get 69%. The gap is small, repeatable, and not explained by geography.

What you are looking at

Every monitored site in the scheme, placed by the similarity of its satellite habitat signature — not by where it is. Neighbours here look alike from orbit; they may be 600 km apart.

Read it carefully

76% is real but modest. It is a long way from a clean split. Nothing here should be read as certainty the number does not carry.

The baseline is 69%, not 50%. There are {{ nUkbms }} UKBMS sites against {{ nWcbs }} WCBS, so always guessing "UKBMS" already scores 69%. The lift is the finding.

Geography was ruled out. A classifier handed only latitude and longitude lands exactly on baseline, so the embedding is not quietly encoding "this is southern England".

Correlation, not mechanism. This tracks where volunteers chose to walk. It is not evidence that the model understands butterflies.

Why it matters anyway. Habitat quality is normally established by someone walking the ground. A signal this cheap — free imagery, no fieldwork — could narrow down where to look first, at national scale, before anyone sets out.

TESSERA compresses a year of Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 imagery into 128 numbers per 10 m pixel. It was trained on no ecological data at all. The question is only whether that representation happens to carry habitat information — and the UKBMS site network, with its {{ n }} sites and half a century of counts, is an unusually good way to ask.
