An AI looked at satellite images of the UK. It has never seen a butterfly, never read an ecology textbook — it only knows patterns of light bouncing off the land. Can it still tell where butterflies are likely to thrive?

What does the AI actually remember?

Not the photo itself — a fingerprint of it. The same way facial recognition doesn't store every pixel of your face, it stores the pattern of features that make your face yours.

satellite photo
→
landscape fingerprint

This fingerprint — called an embedding — is 128 numbers per location. It doesn't say "tree" or "field." It just says: this patch of land looks like this, not like that.

What the AI actually learned

Not butterflies. Landscapes. Somewhere in those 128 numbers, it discovered structure like:

Woodland edges
Mixed grassland
Flower-rich margins
Damp, sheltered ground

These happen to be exactly the places butterflies love. Nobody told the AI that — it found the pattern on its own, from the shape of the land alone.

The proof

2,921 real UK survey sites. Half chosen by volunteers for known good habitat, half dropped at random. A simple classifier was shown only the satellite fingerprint of each site — no map, no coordinates — and asked to guess which kind it was looking at.

76%

And it isn't just memorizing where each site sits on the map — a version given only latitude and longitude scored no better than the flat guess. It's reading the land, not the location.

How it works, briefly

01

Sample. For each survey site, pull the satellite embedding TESSERA already computed for that patch of land in 2022 — 128 numbers.

02

Label. Mark each site as volunteer-chosen habitat or random square — the only "answer key" the model gets.

03

Classify. Train a simple model to guess the label from the 128 numbers alone, tested on sites it never saw during training.

04

Rule out shortcuts. Check a location-only model can't do the same job — if it could, the AI would just be memorizing geography, not reading landscape.
